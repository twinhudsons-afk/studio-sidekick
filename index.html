01

Start here

If you read nothing else, read this section. It's the shape of the whole thing.

The one idea everything else hangs off

Your rig makes sound in two completely different ways, and almost every question you'll have is really a question about which one you're in.

Audio is recorded sound — your voice through the microphone, or the piano through a cable. It arrives through the Scarlett. Once it's recorded it's fixed, like a photograph.

MIDI is not sound at all. It's a list of instructions: this note, this hard, at this moment. It arrives from your keyboard straight into the Mac, and the computer makes the actual sound. Because it's only instructions, you can change the notes, the tempo, the key, or swap a piano for a trumpet afterwards — and nothing has to be re-recorded.

Why this matters Write your parts as MIDI wherever you can. It stays editable forever. Save audio recording for the things only you can make — your voice, mainly.

Two separate paths that only meet inside FL Studio. Once you can picture this, half the confusing problems explain themselves.

Round the back

  • USB-C — the one cable to the Mac. Carries the audio both ways and powers the whole box.

The three cable types you'll touch

  • XLR — three pins, locks with a click. Microphone → Scarlett input 1. Carries the 48V power too.
  • Jack (¼-inch) — like a big headphone plug. Piano line-out → Scarlett input 2.
  • USB — keyboard → Mac, and Scarlett → Mac. Data, not sound-in-a-wire — which is why the keyboard one can't "sound bad".

First-time setup

The setup checklist

Mac only — catches everyone

Microphone permission, in detail

macOS blocks every app from hearing any input device until you say yes. FL Studio doesn't warn you about this — it just records silence, which looks exactly like broken hardware.

  1. Apple menu → System Settings

  2. Privacy & Security → Microphone

  3. Find FL Studio and switch it on

  4. Quit FL Studio completely and open it again. This step is not optional.

If FL Studio isn't in that list at all On Apple Silicon Macs (M1–M4) FL Studio sometimes never asks for permission, so it never appears. The fix is to start FL Studio in Rosetta mode once — right-click the app in Applications, choose Get Info, tick Open using Rosetta, and launch it. macOS asks for microphone permission on startup. The permission is remembered separately for each mode, so if you switch back you may need to grant it again.

Audio settings, and what the numbers mean

Options → Audio Settings. On a Mac there's no ASIO — macOS uses Core Audio, which is built in and works out of the box.

Buffer length

How big a chunk of sound the Mac works on at once. Small chunks mean less delay but more strain; big chunks mean more delay but smoother playback.

What you're doingBufferRoughly
Playing or recording live128 – 2563 – 6 ms delay
Mixing a busy project512 – 2048Delay stops mattering
How to find your best setting Start at 512. Play the project and watch the underrun counter in Audio Settings. If it stays on zero, halve it to 256 and try again. Your best buffer is the lowest number that keeps that counter on zero. Turn it back up when you're mixing.

Sample rate

44100 Hz or 48000 Hz. Either is fine. Pick one and never change it mid-project — changing it can throw your recordings out of sync.

Playing the keyboard

How the keyboard becomes a piano, a trumpet, or a drum kit.

The Channel Rack, and the rule that governs it

The Channel Rack is the list of instruments in your project. Your keyboard plays whichever one is currently selected. Click a different one and the same keys make a different sound. That's the entire routing system.

WordMeans
ChannelOne slot in the rack, holding one instrument
Plugin (or virtual instrument)The software that makes the sound — FLEX, FPC
Preset (or patch)The specific sound inside it — Grand Piano, Trumpet Section
One channel is one sound A song with piano, bass, drums and strings is four channels, played one at a time. You record each part separately and they stack up.

Surprises everyone the first time

Drums work completely differently

On a trumpet or piano preset, every key is the same instrument at a different pitch. On a drum kit, every key is a different drum:

So you play drums with a small handful of low keys, not with melodies. Different kits shuffle this slightly — play up the bottom two octaves once and you'll learn any kit in about thirty seconds. The complete kit — every drum labelled with its job — is drawn out in Making beats.

You don't have to play drums at all The step sequencer lets you click drums in, one square at a time — see Making beats. It's easier and more accurate than playing them.

Recording what you play — or drawing it

Notes get into FL two ways: play them on the keyboard, or draw them as blocks in the Piano roll (F7). Both end up identical and editable:

  1. Click the channel you want to play, so it's selected.

  2. Set the tempo (the BPM number at the top). Do this before you record, not after.

  3. Turn the metronome on so you play in time.

  4. Press the record button, then play. FL counts you in.

  5. Wrong notes? Open the Piano roll and drag them where they should be. Nothing needs re-recording — this is the whole point of MIDI.

Slow it right down If a part is too fast to play cleanly, halve the tempo, record it comfortably, then put the tempo back. The notes speed up perfectly. Everybody does this and nobody mentions it.
Pin this one — it's the map everything else uses

The keyboard map: which keys do which job

A full piano has 88 keys, but a song only uses them in three working zones. Learn the zones once and you always know where your hands belong:

  • BASS ZONE (the low left). One note at a time, played with the left hand — the roots of your chords, or a long 808. Two notes down here at once turns to mud; the low end is a one-note-at-a-time neighbourhood.
  • CHORD ZONE (the middle). Where full chords sound rich without being boomy. Piano chords, warm pads, guitar strums — the bed of the song lives here, played around middle C and just below.
  • MELODY ZONE (the upper right). Riffs, hooks, lead lines — and crucially, where a singer's voice already lives. Which cuts both ways, next point.
Matching the keys to her voice Sing a comfortable note, then hunt along the melody zone until a key plays the same note — that's where her voice sits on this map. Two rules follow: write melodies in that region so they're singable, and keep busy instruments out of it while she's singing — a piano riff on her exact notes fights her for the same space. Play instrumental answers an octave up, or save them for the gaps between vocal lines. (Choosing the whole song's key to flatter her voice is section 09.)
Your keyboard is a window onto this map A 25- or 49-key keyboard shows only a slice of the picture above. The OCTAVE + / − buttons on it slide the window up and down the map — the same physical key can be bass, chord or melody depending on where the window sits. If a preset suddenly sounds like a chipmunk or an earthquake, the window is just parked in the wrong zone: tap OCTAVE the other way.
Start to finish, no steps skipped

Making a MIDI sound: the complete routine

This is the full ritual for getting ANY instrument sound out of the keyboard — the same seven moves every time, whatever the sound. Once this is automatic, the whole instrument side of FL is yours:

  1. Open the Channel Rack (F6) and click the + at the bottom → FLEX. A new channel appears holding FLEX.

  2. Pick the sound. In FLEX, browse the categories on the left (Keys, Guitar, Bass, Brass, Strings, Pads, Synth, 808) or type in the search box. Single-click a preset to hear it demoed; double-click to load it. The category names ARE the zone guide: Bass and 808 presets expect the bass zone, Keys/Pads expect the chord zone, most Lead/Synth presets expect the melody zone.

  3. Select the channel — click its name so the little lime strip to its right lights up. The keyboard plays whichever channel carries that strip, always. Silence when you play almost always means the wrong channel is selected.

  4. Find the zone. Play a few keys. Wrong register? OCTAVE + / − on the keyboard until the sound sits where it should (a bass preset down low, a lead up high).

  5. Play with dynamics. The keyboard senses how hard you press — velocity. On good presets soft playing doesn't just get quieter, it gets warmer and rounder, and hard playing gets brighter and sharper. A felt-piano pad stroked softly and hammered hard are two different instruments. Try both before deciding a preset is wrong.

  6. Record it: tempo first, metronome on, press record, play (the full checklist is the card above). Keep held notes held — the note lasts as long as your finger stays down, and short stabby presses on a pad or string sound is the most common beginner tell.

  7. Tidy in the Piano roll (F7): drag wrong notes onto the right ones, drag note ends to fix lengths — and if the timing is loose, press Alt+Q (quantize) to snap the starts to the grid. For anything that should feel human, quantize at strength ~50% rather than 100% so it tightens without going robotic.

Then repeat, one channel per sound Piano chords first (chord zone), bass next (bass zone, following the kick), melody line last (melody zone, in the gaps around the voice). Three passes of the same seven moves and you've built the whole instrumental bed of a song.

The problem everyone hits on day one

Hearing yourself twice

You sing, and your voice comes back doubled, hollow or slightly echoey. Nothing is broken — you're hearing yourself down two paths at once:

The fix Leave DIRECT MONITOR on at the Scarlett, and turn input monitoring off in FL Studio for that mixer track. Now you hear yourself with no delay at all, and the backing track through the same headphones.

Actually recording a take

  1. In the Mixer, click an empty track. At the top, set its input to Solo Input 1.

  2. Arm it — the record dot at the bottom of that mixer track.

  3. Press record in the toolbar, choose Audio into the playlist, and sing.

  4. Do it three or four times onto different tracks. Choose later. Nobody's first take is their best one.

Headphones, always Never record with speakers on. The microphone picks up the backing track and glues it permanently to your vocal, where it can never be removed.

Small things that make a big difference

  • Stand about a hand's width from the mic, and stay there. Moving closer and further changes your volume mid-take.
  • Sing slightly across the mic rather than straight into it. Kills most popping p and b sounds for free.
  • A pop shield costs very little and does the rest of that job.
  • Soft furnishings beat bare rooms. Near a curtain or a wardrobe of clothes sounds noticeably better than the middle of an empty room.
  • Record the loud chorus first. Set your gain for the loudest thing you'll sing — quiet parts always fit underneath, but a loud part set too hot is ruined.

Shaping your voice

A recorded vocal is raw material. These are all the ways FL Studio can shape it — from a barely-there polish to a completely different voice. The Shape my voice card at the top walks any of them with you.

Learn this order once — it never changes

The vocal chain — what it is and why it exists

A recording of your voice comes out of the mic dry: no space around it, loud notes loud, quiet words vanishing, the room rumble still in it. Nobody releases that. Every vocal you've ever heard on a record has passed through a row of small tools first — and that row is the chain. Think of water through pipes: the voice flows in one end, each tool changes exactly one thing, and it comes out the other end sounding like a record.

Each stage fixes one problem, and each hands a cleaner voice to the next — which is why the order never changes: EQ fixes the tone, compression fixes the evenness, reverb adds the space, delay adds echoes when you want them. The four cards below take each stage apart properly.

Where it physically lives: press F9, click the vocal's mixer track, and load each effect into the slots on the right — the voice enters at slot 1 and flows downward, so top-to-bottom is the order:

Why this order — the one-line version Every tool reacts to what it's fed. Fix the tone first and the compressor squeezes voice, not rumble; even the level and the reverb echoes clarity, not mud. Reverb applied to a muddy, uneven voice just gives you muddy, uneven echoes — reverb is a photocopier for whatever you feed it.
Want it as a guided tour? The Shape my voice card at the top has a "What IS the vocal chain?" path — five screens, one per stage, in plain words, then it builds the whole chain on your actual vocal with you.

EQ — tidy the tone first

Every sound is made of frequencies — lows you feel in your chest, highs that sparkle — and an EQ is a volume control for each region separately. A voice spreads across this whole map, and each region has its own personality:

Load Fruity parametric EQ 2 into slot 1. Four moves cover almost every voice — each one is a block on the map above:

  • Cut everything below ~90 Hz. There's no voice down there — only mic-stand rumble and room boom.
  • A small dip around 250 Hz if the voice sounds boxy or muddy.
  • A gentle lift around 3 kHz for presence — the "closer to the speaker" frequencies.
  • A small shelf up from 10 kHz for air. (If you recorded with the Scarlett's AIR button on, you may already have enough.)
Small moves2–3 dB is a real EQ move. If you can hear the change instantly and obviously, it's probably too much.

Compression — even it out

A compressor automatically turns down the loud moments so the whole take sits at a steady level — it's why record vocals feel like they're resting on top of the music without ever disappearing.

  • Load Fruity Limiter in slot 2 and switch it to COMP mode.
  • Lower the threshold until the meter shows about 3–4 dB of reduction on loud lines.
  • If the voice starts audibly "pumping", back the threshold off.
Room · plate · hall

Reverb — put the voice in a space

Sing in a bathroom and thousands of tiny echoes blur into a wash — that wash is reverb, and the plugin fakes it so a bedroom recording can live in any room you like. This is what a reverb is actually made of, and what each knob controls:

Load Fruity Reeverb 2 in slot 3. The WET knob is the one that matters — it sets how tall the reverb cloud is next to the dry voice. Vocals usually sit between 15% and 25%: you should feel the space more than hear it.

  • Verses drier, choruses wetter — automate WET up for the chorus and the song opens like a door.
  • PRE-DELAY around 20 ms keeps the words clear by letting each syllable land before its reverb starts.
  • Fast songs need short decays (under ~1.5 s) or the reverb smears over the next line. Slow ballads can carry 2.5 s+ hall tails.

Delay — echoes in time

Load Fruity Delay 3 in slot 4 when you want echo as an effect rather than a space. Two settings cover most songs: a single fast slapback for attitude, or a tempo-synced 1/4-note echo for size and dreaminess.

The throw The pro move: automate the delay's mix up for just the last word of a phrase, so that one word echoes off into the gap. Instant record-sounding production.

Pitch — correction and the tuned-pop sound

Two different jobs, two tools:

  • Fixing a note or two: NewTone — opens the recording as editable notes; drag the flat one up. Surgical, invisible.
  • The modern pop sheen: Pitcher on the mixer track, set to your song's key. Slow correction speed = invisible polish; fast = the hard, quantised pop shimmer, as a deliberate style.
Your copy is Producer Edition Pitcher and NewTone only come with Signature Edition and up, so they're not in your copy. The working routes on Producer: sing the wobbly line again (free, usually best), or install a free tuner plugin — Graillon 2 Free (Auburn Sounds) or MAutoPitch (MeldaProduction), both Mac AU, both with the same two controls (KEY + speed). After installing: Add → More plugins → Manage plugins → Find plugins, then load it after compression, before reverb. Pitcher can also be bought on its own from Image-Line, much cheaper than the full upgrade.

The biggest chorus-thickener costs nothing but a second take. Where every voice sits:

  • The harmony: sing the line again a third higher — every note moved two letter-names up the scale:
  • Only in the chorus. Verses stay one voice — the arrival of the doubles IS the chorus lift.

07

Building a song

You sing and you play. That's already the hard part. This is the order that makes the rest fall into place.

This order is the point

Build it in this sequence

  1. Tempo and key come from your voice. Sing the chorus. Find the tempo that feels right and the key where the highest note is comfortable — not just reachable. Set both in FL first.

  2. The drum beat. One bar in the step sequencer is your engine — see Making beats.

  3. Piano chords over the beat. Four chords, one bar each, is a complete verse. This is the spine of the song.

  4. Then bass. Just the lowest note of each chord, landing with the kick. It sounds too simple to matter and it is the single biggest step towards sounding like a real record.

  5. Then guitar and colour — chops, arpeggios, pads, shakers. One new sound per section, and the middle stays clear for your voice.

The guided version, in three levels The Build a song card at the top walks this whole sequence interactively — Beginner (first full track), Intermediate (guitar, layers, fills, the pre-chorus build), Advanced (tempo changes, transitions, automation). Each level ends with a quick self-test, and each assumes the one before.
Why the order matters This is exactly why loops from sample sites rarely fit. They were made in someone else's key at someone else's tempo, so you end up bending your song to fit a loop. Build the parts to your song instead and the problem disappears entirely.
So it's not one beat the whole way through

Making the song move

A track that never changes speed or weight reads as amateur long before anyone notices the sounds. Three tools, cheapest first:

1. Perceived speed — change the drums, not the BPM

Hats to 16ths and a busier kick make a chorus feel faster at the same tempo. Moving the snare from beats 2 & 4 to just beat 3 makes the same BPM feel half-speed and heavy — devastating for a bridge or the first half of a final chorus. This is where almost all "speeding up" in records actually happens.

2. Real tempo changes — automate the BPM itself

Right-click the BPM display → Create automation clip. A ramped +4–6 BPM into the final chorus reads as excitement; easing the last bars down 20–30 BPM ends the song like a live band. Set tempo automation before recording vocals — audio doesn't stretch as gracefully as MIDI.

3. Section changes — one event per boundary

Every border needs one thing to change: a drop-out bar, a fill, a reverse crash, a filter opening, a layer in or out. The verse beat should be the chorus beat with pieces deleted, and the last bar before each chorus is the fill bar. All drawn out in Making beats and taught step-by-step in the Advanced walk.

The last ten minutes

Mastering, the beginner version

Mastering is the final polish that makes the finished song loud and even enough to sit next to other records. It happens on the Master strip in the Mixer, using its effect slots:

  1. Click the Master strip in the Mixer (F9), then load into its slots on the right:

  2. Slot 1 — Fruity Parametric EQ 2. Two gentle moves at most: a small dip around 200–300 Hz if the song sounds muddy, a small lift on the top shelf for air. If a move is obvious, it's too big.

  3. Slot 2 — Fruity Limiter. The default limiter setting catches peaks. Raise its gain until your song is about as loud as a reference track you love — and stop the instant it starts sounding squashed or pumping.

  4. Watch the master meter — it should dance near the top without slamming red. Red = permanent distortion in the export.

  5. File → Export → WAV for the keeper; MP3 320 for sharing. Play the export on phone speakers and headphones — if it survives both, it's done.

Mastering can't fix the mix A crowded arrangement, a clipped vocal, or a muddy low end has to be fixed where it happened — mastering only polishes what's already working. If the master needs big moves, the problem is upstream.

Song structure, with bar counts

A bar is four beats. Almost all pop music is built in blocks of 4, 8 and 16 bars, and following that is what makes an arrangement feel finished rather than random.

PartBarsWhat it's doing
Intro4 – 8Sets the mood. Often just the chords.
Verse 116Tells the story. Keep it sparse — you need somewhere to go.
Pre-chorus8Optional. Builds tension into the chorus.
Chorus16The big one. Everything comes in.
Verse 216Same as verse 1, slightly fuller.
Chorus16Again.
Bridge8Something different — new chords, a drop to just voice and piano.
Final chorus16Biggest version. Add a harmony line.
Outro4 – 8Wind it down.
Arranging is mostly subtraction A chorus doesn't feel big because you added things to it — it feels big because you took things out of the verse before it. If your chorus isn't lifting, thin out the verse rather than piling more onto the chorus.

Patterns and the Playlist

FL splits songwriting into two jobs, and understanding the split saves a lot of confusion.

  • A Pattern is a short musical idea — a four-bar chord loop, a drum beat, a bass line. You make these in the Channel Rack and Piano roll.
  • The Playlist is where you drag those patterns out along a timeline to build the actual song.

Make a verse pattern, a chorus pattern, a drum pattern, then arrange them in the Playlist. Change the pattern once and every copy of it in the song updates.

Name your patterns as you make them Right-click the pattern selector and rename. "Pattern 7" means nothing by tomorrow; "Verse chords" costs two seconds.

Making beats

Everything about building the drums and low end under your songs — the tool, the patterns, and the tricks that make a beat feel human. Or let the Make me a beat wizard at the top build one with you.

The step sequencer: FL's beat machine

Beats in FL are made by clicking squares, not playing keys. Each drum gets a row of 16 squares — one bar, four squares per beat. Click a square and the drum hits there. That's the whole mechanism.

  1. Open the Channel Rack (F6). Kick, Clap, Hat and Snare channels are there in a new project.

  2. Click squares to place hits. Right-click removes them.

  3. Press Space. The pattern loops so you hear every change instantly.

  4. To swap a drum sound: right-click the channel name → Replace, or drag a sound from the Browser onto it.

The squares are the same grids drawn below Every pattern in the library underneath is drawn exactly as the step sequencer shows it. Copy the filled squares and you have the beat.
Meet the whole kit before you build with it

The moment that makes or breaks the song

When the chorus hits: the drum lift

A chorus should feel bigger and faster — but on records the tempo almost never changes. The felt speed-up is the drums getting denser. Here is the whole manoeuvre, drawn:

  1. The fill announces it. In the last bar of the verse: snare on steps 13-14-15-16, hats cleared out of the way. (Or a tom roll falling low→high into the chorus.)

  2. Crash on arrival. Step 1 of the first chorus bar, once. It rings over the first two beats and stamps "NEW SECTION" on the song.

  3. Hats double. Verse hats on every other cell become chorus hats on every cell. This single move is the "speed-up" your ear hears — same tempo, twice the motion.

  4. The open hat breathes on the off-beats (3, 7, 11, 15) — the chorus gains air as well as speed.

  5. One or two extra kicks (try steps 4 and 12) so the floor moves more underneath her longest notes.

  6. Alternative drama: instead of a fill, delete everything for the last half-bar of the verse — one beat of silence, then the chorus lands on top of the crash. Free goosebumps.

If the song genuinely speeds up Some songs do push the tempo at the chorus, on purpose. Do it with automation, not by hand: right-click the BPM display → Create automation clip, and raise it 2–4 BPM at most into the final chorus — a lift that size reads as excitement. More reads as a mistake. The drawn walkthrough is in Building a song under tempo changes.
Slow R&B lifts differently At 60–75 BPM a full hat-doubling can trample the mood. Lift gently instead: double the shaker rather than the hats, add the crash, add ONE extra kick — and let the vocal stack (doubles and harmonies arriving) do the heavy lifting. In this genre the chorus gets fuller more than it gets faster.
The trap & drill signature

Hi-hat rolls

That rapid-fire tikatikatika in trap and drill isn't a special sound — it's the same hi-hat placed on smaller subdivisions for a moment. In FL: right-click the Hat channel → Fill each 2 steps for 8ths, then for a roll, zoom the grid finer (the little dropdown sets steps per beat) or open the Piano roll and paint 1/32 notes for half a beat.

Two rules make rolls sound professional rather than chaotic: use them sparingly — one roll at the end of a 4- or 8-bar phrase, not constantly — and ramp the velocity so the roll swells in, quiet to loud.

The kick and the bass are one instrument

The most common reason a beginner beat sounds thin: the kick and the bass ignore each other. They share the same low territory, so they have to take turns or lock together.

  • The bass notes come from your chords. Whatever chord is playing, the bass plays its root note. C chord → C bass. You already made these decisions when you chose the chords.
  • Simplest lock: bass note lands exactly on each kick hit. Tight, punchy, works everywhere.
  • 808 style (trap, R&B): the bass IS the low boom — a long 808 note per chord, sliding between roots. In FLEX, search "808".
  • If the low end sounds muddy, the kick and a long bass note are overlapping. Shorten the bass note so it gets out of the kick's way, and it cleans up instantly.

Making it human

  • Velocity is the big one. Real drummers never hit twice identically. In the Channel Rack, the graph editor button (top-right) shows a bar under each step — drag some hi-hats quieter. Alternating loud/soft hats transforms a beat on its own.
  • Ghost notes: very quiet snare hits between the main ones (the R&B pattern above shows them faded). Barely audible, hugely felt.
  • Swing: the knob at the top of the Channel Rack pushes off-beats late. 15–25% makes a stiff beat groove; 0% suits driving pop; more turns it into a shuffle.
  • A fill every 8 bars: snare on steps 13-14-15-16 of the last bar before a section change announces the chorus.
  • Drop the drums out for one bar before a big chorus. Free drama.
Rule one for a singer's beats

The beat serves the voice

You're not making instrumentals — you're making room for a singer. Where each element sits, high to low:

  • The middle belongs to the voice. Kick and 808 live low, hats and shakers live high — they frame the voice without touching it. Busy mid-range instruments (big synth chords, dense guitars) are what fight a vocal.
  • Busy voice, simple beat. During fast or wordy vocal lines, drums stay plain. Save the rolls and fills for the gaps between vocal phrases — that's call and response, and it's what pro productions do constantly.
  • Verse thinner, chorus fuller. Copy the beat pattern, then remove pieces for the verse version: maybe just kick and a shaker. The chorus hits harder because the verse got out of its way.

Keys & chords

Choosing a key that flatters your voice, and knowing which chords belong in it.

Do this before writing anything else

Finding your key

The key isn't a musical technicality — it decides whether your chorus sounds effortless or strained. Choose it for your voice, not for what's easy to play.

  1. Sing the chorus melody in whatever key falls out naturally. Don't think about it.

  2. Find the highest note in it. Can you hold it comfortably — and would you still be able to on the fourth take at eleven at night? If not, the key is too high.

  3. Move the whole thing down a step or two and try again. Keep going until the top note is comfortable rather than merely possible.

  4. Then check the lowest note. If it's disappearing into nothing, you've gone too far — back up one.

The test that matters Your best key is where the highest note in the chorus is comfortable and the lowest note is still audible. That window is usually only two or three keys wide, and finding it is the difference between a vocal that soars and one that sounds like hard work.

The chords in each key

Every key has six chords that naturally belong together. Pick any of them in any order and it will sound right. Roman numerals are just shorthand for "the first chord", "the fourth chord" — they let a progression be described once and used in any key.

A trick worth stealing Use the same four chords for the verse and the chorus, but start the chorus on a different one. Same harmony, and it feels like the song has lifted into a new place.

Making chords sound good rather than correct

  • Don't play every chord in the same hand position. Moving each chord to whichever shape is nearest your last one — inversions — is what stops a progression sounding like a beginner exercise.
  • Leave the bottom to the bass. If a bass part is playing the root note, your left hand doesn't need to. Play the chords higher up and the whole thing opens out.
  • Hold chords through the bar in verses; play them more rhythmically in choruses. Same chords, more energy.
  • Add the 7th for colour. Am7 instead of Am, Cmaj7 instead of C. One extra note, instantly more grown-up.

Fix it

The guided fixers at the top of the page ask you questions and narrow down — these are the same fixes flat on the page, causes most-likely first.

No sound at all

Nine times out of ten it's one of the first three.

  1. Are the headphones in the Scarlett? Not the Mac. And is the MONITOR knob turned up?

  2. Is FL pointed at the Scarlett? Options → Audio Settings → Device → Scarlett Solo USB.

  3. Is the Mac's output set to the Scarlett? System Settings → Sound → Output.

  4. Is the master volume up in FL? Top-right of the toolbar, and the master track in the Mixer.

  5. Is anything muted or soloed? A soloed channel silences everything else and it's easy to leave on by accident.

  6. Restart FL Studio. If the Scarlett was plugged in after FL opened, it may not have been found.

The keyboard does nothing

Almost always number 1 or number 2.

  1. Have you enabled it? Options → MIDI Settings, click the keyboard in the Input list, click Enable. It sits there listed but silent until you do — which looks identical to being broken.

  2. Did you plug it in after opening FL Studio? FL only looks for keyboards at startup. Close and reopen FL, or use the rescan option in MIDI Settings.

  3. Does the Port column show a dash? Set it to 0.

  4. Set the controller type to Generic Controller if it isn't already.

  5. Have you selected a channel? The keyboard plays the currently selected channel. None selected, no sound.

  6. Try a different cable. Charge-only USB cables are extremely common and produce exactly this symptom.

  7. Check the Mac sees it at all. Applications → Utilities → Audio MIDI Setup → Window → Show MIDI Studio. If the keyboard isn't there either, it's the cable or port, not FL Studio.

The microphone records silence

On a Mac, this is nearly always permissions.

  1. Grant microphone permission. System Settings → Privacy & Security → Microphone → FL Studio on → then quit and reopen FL Studio. Skipping the restart is why people think this didn't work.

  2. Not in that list at all? Launch FL once in Rosetta mode so macOS prompts — see First-time setup.

  3. Is 48V on? Your RØDE is a condenser — without 48V it's almost completely silent.

  4. Is the gain up? The ring around Gain 1 should light green when you sing.

  5. Is the mixer track's input set? It must point at Solo Input 1.

  6. Is the track armed? The record dot at the bottom of the mixer track.

  7. Do you have Fruity Edition? It cannot record audio at all. Help → About to check.

Crackling, popping or stuttering

The computer can't keep up. Give it more time or less work.

  1. Raise the buffer length. Options → Audio Settings → try 512, then 1024. Fixes it most of the time.

  2. Close everything else. Browsers with many tabs are the usual culprit.

  3. Plug the Mac into power. On battery, macOS throttles the processor and audio suffers first.

  4. Is the Scarlett on a hub? Plug it straight into the Mac.

  5. Freeze or bounce finished parts so their plugins stop using processor.

A delay between pressing a key and hearing it

This is latency, and it's a setting, not a fault.

  1. Lower the buffer length to 128 or 256. Under about 10 ms feels instant.

  2. Turn off heavy effects while recording, especially reverb and anything on the master. Put them back afterwards.

  3. For singing, use DIRECT MONITOR on the Scarlett instead. That path skips the computer completely, so there is no delay to fix.

Your voice sounds doubled or echoey while singing

One cause, one fix.

  1. You're monitoring twice. DIRECT MONITOR on the Scarlett and input monitoring in FL are both feeding you the same voice, a few milliseconds apart.

  2. Turn input monitoring off in FL for that track, keep DIRECT MONITOR on. Full explanation in Recording your voice.

The recording is distorted or crunchy

Too loud somewhere. Find where.

  1. Turn Gain 1 down. If the ring goes red while you sing, that's clipping and it's permanent — no plugin removes it.

  2. Is INST on for the piano? It should be off — it's a guitar boost and distorts line-level signals.

  3. Aim for green, never red. Quieter is always recoverable; distorted never is.

A downloaded loop doesn't fit the song

It never will, and that's not your fault.

  1. It was made at a different tempo in a different key. FL can stretch and pitch it, but it degrades and still won't sit properly.

  2. Look for MIDI, or single hits, rather than audio loops. Both adapt to your song automatically.

  3. Better still, build the part yourself — Making beats and Building a song are exactly this.

Words explained

Every term you'll run into, in plain English.

808
A long, booming bass note — named after the Roland TR-808 drum machine that invented the sound. In trap and R&B the 808 is both the bass line and part of the drums.
AIR
The Solo's built-in brightness lift for vocals — a gentle boost to the sparkle frequencies, done in the hardware before recording.
Audio interface
The box between your instruments and computer — your Scarlett Solo. Converts real sound into computer data and back.
Bar
Four beats. Music is counted in bars, and songs are built in blocks of 4, 8 and 16 of them.
BPM
Beats per minute — the tempo. A ballad is around 70, most pop sits between 100 and 130.
Buffer
How big a chunk of sound your Mac processes at once. Small = less delay, more strain; large = more delay, smoother playback.
Channel Rack
FL Studio's list of the instruments in your project, each with its row of step-sequencer squares. Your keyboard plays whichever one is selected.
Clipping
Recording something so loud it distorts. Permanent — nothing repairs it. The red gain ring is the warning.
Condenser / dynamic mic
Two kinds of microphone. A condenser (your RØDE) is sensitive and detailed and needs 48V power. A dynamic is tougher, needs no power, and forgives a noisy room.
Core Audio
The Mac's built-in audio system — why your Scarlett works with no drivers. The Windows equivalent, ASIO, doesn't exist on a Mac and you don't need it.
DAW
Digital Audio Workstation — the software you make music in. FL Studio is one; so are Logic and Ableton.
Direct Monitor
The Scarlett button that sends your voice straight to your headphones without the computer round-trip, so there's no delay while you sing.
Gain
How much the Scarlett amplifies your microphone. Too low is noisy, too high distorts. Green ring good, red ring too late.
Ghost note
A very quiet drum hit between the main ones — barely heard, strongly felt. The cheapest way to make a beat groove.
Half-time
A drum feel where the snare lands half as often, making the same tempo feel slow and heavy. Trap is built on it.
Headroom
The gap between your loudest moment and the point of distortion. Leaving some is what stops a great take being ruined.
Key
The home note of a song and the family of chords built around it. Choose it to suit your voice.
Compression
Automatically turning down the loud moments so the whole performance sits at an even level. The reason record vocals never disappear behind the band.
Delay
Distinct echoes of the voice, usually timed to the song’s tempo. An effect you hear, where reverb is a space you feel.
Latency
The delay between doing something and hearing it. Set by the buffer. Under 10 ms feels instant; over 20 ms feels wrong.
Limiter
An effect that stops sound going past a ceiling, letting you raise the overall loudness safely. The last thing on the Master when mastering — Fruity Limiter in FL.
Mastering
The final polish on the finished song — gentle EQ and limiting on the Master strip so it's loud and even enough to sit beside other records. Minutes, not hours, at this level.
MIDI
Note instructions, not sound. Endlessly editable — change the notes, tempo, key or instrument afterwards without re-recording.
Mixer
Where every sound's volume, effects and routing live. Audio recording happens here too.
One-shot
A single recorded hit — one kick, one snare — that you sequence yourself. The opposite of a loop, and the right thing to download.
Pattern
A short musical block in FL — a drum beat, a chord loop. You arrange copies of these in the Playlist to make the song.
Phantom power / 48V
Power the Scarlett sends up the mic cable to a condenser microphone. Essential for your RØDE.
Piano roll
The grid where MIDI notes appear as blocks you can drag, lengthen and delete. Where you fix wrong notes and paint hat rolls.
Playlist
FL's timeline, where patterns and audio are arranged into the finished song.
Plugin
Software that makes or changes sound. FLEX and FPC are instruments; reverb and compressors are effects.
Preset / patch
A specific saved sound inside a plugin — "Grand Piano", "Warm Pad".
Reverb
The sound of a space — room, plate or hall — around the voice. The WET knob sets how much; vocals usually want 15–25%.
Pitch correction
Nudging sung notes onto exact pitch — invisibly (slow, NewTone/Pitcher) or as the hard quantised pop effect (fast Pitcher).
Sample rate
How many times per second the sound is measured. 44100 or 48000. Pick one, don't change it mid-project.
Step sequencer
The rows of 16 squares in the Channel Rack. Click a square, the drum plays there. How most beats get made.
Swing
Pushing the off-beats slightly late so a rigid beat grooves. A knob at the top of the Channel Rack.
Take
One recorded attempt. Record several; choose later.
Velocity
How hard a note was played. Varying it is what stops programmed parts sounding robotic.
XLR
The three-pin cable microphones use. Locks into place with a click.

Habits that save whole afternoons

  • Save with a new version number each session — song v1, song v2. When you break something at midnight, yesterday's version is still there.
  • Name every pattern and mixer track as you create it. Two seconds each, and it's the difference between a project you can come back to and one you can't.
  • Set tempo and key before you record anything. Changing them later is possible for MIDI and painful for audio.
  • Keep one project as your template — audio settings, drum channels, a piano channel, mixer tracks already named. Start every song from it.
  • Take your headphones off every half hour. Ears stop hearing accurately long before they feel tired, and mixes made on tired ears are always too bright.
  • Finish things. A finished song you don't love teaches you more than a perfect eight bars you never get past.
